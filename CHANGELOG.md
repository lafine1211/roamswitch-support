## AccessScope

A security diagnosis tool for access logs. It is a separate app (Apache-2.0): the Server Edition .deb / .rpm packages carry only the command (`accessscope`; from 1.11.2 they also carry the PersonalSOC and PathScope commands); the Linux client edition and the Mac app carry the command and a window app.

### 0.3.1

- **Fix: the terms for "severity" and "confidence" are now consistent in the Korean and Chinese screens and reports.** In Korean, confidence is now "확신도" throughout (the old "신뢰도" was easy to confuse with "신뢰" in "trusted proxy"). In Simplified and Traditional Chinese, "严重度/嚴重度" is now "严重程度/嚴重程度", the confidence level "有力" is "较可能/較可能", and the severity level "中等" is "中". What is detected and how it is judged do not change.

### 0.3.0

- **It shows what one address did across several logs as a single sequence** (`actor_chain`). An address that appears in two or more logs among web, SSH, PAM, mail, database, HAProxy and fail2ban, and has at least one stage of medium severity or higher, is put in time order, with one evidence line per stage. The only thing that ties the stages together is that the address matches; the text says that this is not evidence of the same person or the same tool. It never suggests a success when no successful login was recorded (it says so when there is no record). Private, loopback and similar addresses, behind which NAT or a proxy can hide many people, are not linked. The overall risk does not count the sequence twice on top of its parts, and the blocklist takes no IP from the sequence itself.
- **It finds logins from an unfamiliar source** (`ssh_new_source_login`, `mail_new_source_login`, `db_new_source_login`). It reports a successful login when the account has a history of normal logins (at least 3 successes from other sources, the first one at least 24 hours earlier) and the source has had no success in the 30 days of log before it. The default is low and hypothesis. It is raised to medium when failures from the same source came first, or when the same address also shows up in other logs as reconnaissance, an attack or brute force. For SSH public-key logins, when the log has the key fingerprint (`SHA256:...`), the finding says whether the key is one the account used before or one it had never used. It does not report an address in the same /24 (IPv4) or /56 (IPv6) as one the account used, nor an address inside the organisation for an unprivileged account. The finding also says that a business trip, a new device, a VPN or an address change could be the reason.
- **It finds differences from the usual** (`web_new_ua`, `web_new_path`, `web_path_shift`). It compares a recent period (by default the 24 hours up to the last line of the log; `--baseline-period` on the command line, `baseline_hours` in the settings file for the app, from 1 hour to 7 days) with everything earlier in the same log. It reports a User-Agent family that is new and carries 100 or more requests from 20 or more IPs, a new path with 100 or more non-404 hits from 10 or more IPs, an existing path whose rate rose 5 times or more, or a 5xx share that went from 5% or less to 20% or more. Severity is always low and confidence is hypothesis, because a new app version, a campaign or a deploy looks the same; the text gives the compared periods and the numbers. When the log covers fewer than two periods, or is small, nothing is reported and the report says why nothing was compared. With `--state` and `--since-last`, salted hashes of the families and paths seen (no log strings) are also kept, so what an earlier run saw is not treated as new.
- **These three detections are heuristics.** The thresholds above (24 hours, two periods, 100 requests, 20 IPs, 30 days, 3 earlier logins and so on) are judgements checked against our own evaluation data, not tuned on real traffic. On your logs they can miss things and raise false alarms. Each finding separates what the log shows from what it cannot show, and none of them declares a compromise. No network access, geolocation (GeoIP) or LLM is used. Two addresses of the same attacker are not linked. fail2ban writes local time without a time zone, so where an event falls in a sequence depends on the time zone of the machine running AccessScope.
- **Suppression rules gained `user`.** Only the three unfamiliar-source login kinds can be suppressed by account name (combined with `kind`). The account of a successful login is a name the server authenticated, but a user name in a failure log can be written by an attacker, so attack detections cannot be suppressed by `user`.
- **It says so when Docker cannot be used.** When `scan --auto --docker` cannot use the Docker route (no `docker` command, the daemon is not running, or no permission), it prints the reason to standard error and in the input list, and the window shows the same sentence under "Logs found on this machine". Nothing is read from Docker then, and it does not retry with `sudo`.
- **The "Diagnose logs" screen is easier to follow.** There is one main flow: choose a file or folder, or press "Find logs on this machine", then diagnose. Logs found are listed by name, such as "nginx access log" or Docker "web", with a checkbox; internal notations such as container IDs are not shown. Logs that could not be read go in a separate section with a one-line remedy, and files that turned out not to be logs go in a closed section. When nothing is found, it gives a reason built from what was checked and the next steps (choose a file, search with an LLM, specify another location). What was checked is collected in a closed "Detailed discovery results" section that can be copied. Docker and the LLM sit in an options row with their explanations folded away (the confirmation screen and the consent flow are unchanged). It is readable in light and dark colours and wraps in a narrow window.
- **The Docker wording in the window no longer uses technical terms.** Command and group names were removed in favour of plain sentences. The command line and the confirmation screen stay technical.
- **Measured effect.** On our own evaluation data, compared with 0.2.0. This stage added 33 attack cases and 20 normal cases (including cases built from real nginx, OpenSSH and fail2ban lines). Overall attack detection stays at 99.1% (1006 of 1015), and hard variants stay at 98.6%. Normal traffic that raised a finding of medium severity or higher is 5 of 312 (5 of 292 in 0.2.0). Normal cases with any finding, low-severity noise included, went from 68 to 71. The three additions are real changes (a traffic spike from a campaign, a new app version, a deploy that renamed paths) shown as low and hypothesis. Analysing 3.3 million lines took about 6% longer and about 1% more memory. This is not a sample of real traffic, so it does not guarantee results on your logs.
- **A test checks that the same log always gives the same result.** An integration test analyses the same logs 25 times in a row and requires the JSON, SARIF, CSV, text, Markdown, state file and blocklists to be identical.
- The added texts are in 10 languages too (the translations are based on machine translation and have not been reviewed by native speakers).

### 0.2.0

- **Far fewer misses and false alarms.** Measured on our own labelled test data (982 attack cases and 292 normal-traffic cases as of 0.2.0; the data grew during the work). Attack detection went from 83.3% to 99.1% overall, and from 15.7% to 98.6% on hard variants such as encoding and obfuscation. The share of normal traffic that raised a finding of medium severity or higher went from 30% to under 2% (5 of 292). These are figures on our own data, not a guarantee of results on your logs.
- **More resistant to evasion.** Before matching, it undoes repeated URL decoding (up to 3 times), alternative Unicode forms (fullwidth forms, overlong UTF-8, `%uXXXX`), SQL comments, `${IFS}` and nested Log4j lookups. The evidence line shown in a report stays the original log line. Numeric spellings of addresses in SSRF URLs (hex, decimal, octal, short forms) are read too.
- **A CVE rule pack.** Exploit attempts that are recognisable from the request line and appear in CISA's KEV (the catalog of known exploited vulnerabilities) are matched against a data table with sources: 64 rules covering 71 CVEs, dated 2026-10-08. The date and rule count appear at the top of the report, in the JSON and in `--version`, and a finding lists the CVEs it matched. A match means the request had the shape of that exploit, not that it worked.
- **Long lines are no longer dropped silently.** A line over 24 KiB is analysed from its first and last parts, and the number of such lines is shown in the findings and the input list.
- **`--trust-forwarded-for`** (off by default). For logs behind a reverse proxy, it takes the client IP from X-Forwarded-For (rightmost or leftmost). The header can be forged, so it is not used by default. It can also be chosen under "Analysis" in Settings.
- **Detections over time windows.** Login brute force (windows of 1 hour, 1 minute and 14 days), login spraying, SSH spraying (`ssh_spray`), distributed probing by IPs that mostly get 404, and site-wide floods (`web_flood_dist`). A success after SSH failures is counted in the preceding 1-hour window too. When sshd writes two lines for one invalid-user attempt, it is counted once.
- **The effect of an attack is no longer judged from the response size alone.** A 2xx to an attack-looking request is rated high, medium or low by where the payload sits and who sent it (a script or a browser). For example, a browser sending it in a search parameter is medium / hypothesis. If normal responses from the same path already come in 3 or more sizes, a different size is not treated as evidence. No rule removes a finding; when severity or confidence is lowered, the reason is added to the details in plain text.
- **Suppression rules.** A TOML file names IPs, User-Agents, paths or finding kinds. A reason is mandatory and an expiry (`expires`) can be set. A high-severity finding is never hidden unless `allow_high = true`. Suppressed findings are not thrown away: their count and reasons appear in the report (`--show-suppressed` lists them). `suppress.toml` in the settings folder is read automatically, and in the window you can add an IP from a finding with "Suppress this IP…".
- **Verified crawlers are told apart.** IPs are checked against the ranges the vendors publish officially (a snapshot from 2026-10-07), without any network access. A verified crawler is left out of the reconnaissance, enumeration, scanner, high-rate and error findings and is counted instead. A client that claims to be a crawler but comes from outside the range is reported as `web_fake_crawler` (low).
- **Comparison with the previous run.** `--state` and `--since-last` store only fingerprints of findings (a hash of kind, IP and classification) with counts and times, and split the result into new, continuing and gone since last time. With `--new-only` and `--fail-on`, the exit code is 3 only when there is a new finding. The state file is 0600 and discarded after 90 days.
- **More kinds of logs can be read.** Authentication logs of sudo, su, PAM and login, fail2ban, HAProxy (HTTP and TCP), AWS ALB and CloudFront, Postfix and Dovecot, and MySQL, MariaDB and PostgreSQL. 16 kinds of findings were added. An IP that fail2ban already banned is noted on the other findings. Checked against logs captured on real Debian 12, Rocky Linux 9, HAProxy, MariaDB, MySQL and PostgreSQL.
- **sshd logs from the macOS unified log.** It runs a fixed command (`/usr/bin/log show`) by absolute path, without a shell and with an empty environment. Only members of the admin group can read it.
- **Docker container logs.** On Linux, `json-file` log files are also searched. `docker logs` is used only when you choose "Also check Docker containers" in the window and confirm the commands and targets shown, or when you pass `--docker` on the command line. The only commands that can be built are `logs` and `ps`; environment variables such as `DOCKER_HOST` are not inherited and the socket is pinned to this machine, so a remote Docker is never contacted.
- **Log discovery shows a result list.** It lists the verdict for each of the standard locations, config files, logs named in configs, journald, the unified log, Docker and LLM suggestions. A log that a config names but that does not exist is shown as such instead of being dropped silently, and when nothing is found the reason is given in a sentence. The list of found logs is saved (0600, up to 200 entries) and restored at the next start together with the time of the last search; every entry is checked again.
- **Change: the "Review what will be sent" button was removed from "Find logs with an LLM" in the window.** The explanation of what is sent stays. `discover --show-prompt` on the command line remains, and the review feature in the LLM analysis panel is unchanged.
- **The instructions to the LLM now say how to reach a conclusion on each finding.** A conclusion is one of "confirmed", "can be ruled out (with the reason taken from facts)" or "undetermined", and something that could not be confirmed is never written as "no problem".
- **Exports.** `--export-blocklist` writes a blocklist for fail2ban, nftables, ipset, nginx, Apache or CSV. It only writes: nothing is run and no traffic is blocked. IPs of official crawler ranges, IPs covered by suppression rules and private or documentation ranges are left out, and the header records the generation date, source logs, criteria, count and a warning. `--format` also offers CSV (with a leading formula character neutralised), SARIF 2.1.0 and JSON. The JSON carries a `schema_version` and follows a versioned specification. The window has "Export…" too.
- **Window updates.** The input log list shows the number of long lines, the number of lines whose client IP came from X-Forwarded-For, and a note for formats that have no header columns. The top of the results shows the CVE rule pack date and rule count and the date of the crawler IP ranges. Settings gained "Analysis".
- **More attack-tool User-Agents.** The ones HexStrike AI announces, httpx, CensysInspect and the api-recon bundled with ARTEX.
- The added texts are in 10 languages too (translations are machine-made and have not been reviewed by native speakers).

### 0.1.0

- **First release.** From web server access logs (Apache and Nginx common/combined, nginx / Caddy / Traefik JSON, gzip) and SSH authentication logs (sshd logs, and journald on Linux) it finds attack attempts and shows them with the evidence log lines, the MITRE ATT&CK technique ID and a confidence level (confirmed, likely, hypothesis). It is read-only: it never changes logs and never raises privileges. The analysis uses neither an LLM nor the network.
- **What it finds**: SQL injection, path traversal, XSS, command and code injection, Log4Shell, Shellshock, template injection, SSRF, exploit paths of known vulnerabilities (Apache 2.4.49/50, PHPUnit, Laravel Ignition, Citrix, FortiOS, Spring4Shell, Confluence, Exchange and others), probing for secret files and admin pages, attack-tool User-Agents, mass attempts on non-existent paths, login brute force and distributed credential stuffing, possible web shell access, chains from reconnaissance to exploitation, high request rates, SSH brute force, username enumeration, a success after failures, root password logins, and instructions to an AI planted in the logs.
- **An HTTP 200 is not treated as success.** If a 2xx response to an attack-looking request is the same size as the normal page, it is reported as "the payload was probably ignored" and does not raise the severity. The overall risk (0 to 100) is weighted per kind of finding, so the number of attacking IPs does not inflate it.
- **It looks for the logs.** Besides standard locations it checks the places named by nginx, Apache, Caddy and Traefik configs (including rotated files) and, on Linux, journald. Optionally an LLM is given only facts about the environment (names of running servers, log locations named in configs, file names such as under `/var/log`; never file contents) and suggests missing locations. A suggestion is shown only after it is verified to be readable as a log, and nothing is read until you pick and add it.
- **Analysis by an LLM (optional, off by default).** Only a summary and a few evidence log lines are passed to an LLM (claude, opencode, codex, agy) to write a report. Raw logs are not sent; secrets are masked and IPs are defanged (or pseudonymized). `--show-prompt` shows exactly what would be sent. Logs are attacker-controlled, so text that looks like instructions to an AI is reported as a finding and the LLM output is treated as an untrusted quotation. It does not run as root. When enabled, the summary may be sent to an external service through the LLM command you chose.
- The Mac app can update itself (Settings → Update). On Linux it is updated by the package.
- The screens and the command line are in 10 languages (translations are machine-made and have not been reviewed by native speakers).

## PathScope

### 0.1.7

- **Updated: the known-vulnerability data is now the CISA KEV catalog 2026.10.04 (data generated 2026-10-05).** This is the data used by the check of installed software versions against known vulnerabilities (0.1.5); until now it was the 2026.10.02 catalog (generated 2026-10-04). The vulnerability entries in the data are the same as before; only the catalog version and the generation date changed. The data date shown in the result is newer, which gives more time before the notice that appears when the data is over 45 days old. The data is bundled with the app, and nothing is looked up over the network during an analysis. There are no other changes.

### 0.1.6

- **Added: when PathScope itself is the subject of a finding, an explanation is added** (macOS). The finding that an app with Full Disk Access can be overwritten by an ordinary user also applies to PathScope itself, which asks you to allow it so that it can read the privacy settings data (TCC.db). The finding is not hidden: it is marked as being about PathScope itself, with what to do (remove the permission in System Settings once the reading is done; making root the owner also works, but the in-app update then asks for the administrator password and the owner may revert after an update). It is shown in both the app and the command line. The text was added in 10 languages (the translations are based on machine translation and have not been checked by native speakers).

### 0.1.5

- **Added: it checks the versions of installed software against known vulnerabilities.** It compares the versions of packages, the running kernel and macOS with known vulnerabilities that were exploited in real attacks (50 entries from the CISA KEV catalog that let a regular user become root), and adds the ones that are not yet fixed to the paths to root. There is one path per package, the unit you fix, listing the matching CVE numbers. The suggested fix is an update command (apt, dnf, zypper, pacman, apk or softwareupdate); nothing is run automatically. "Fix this?" lets you analyze the result of updating without changing anything.
- **Supported environments**: macOS, and on Linux the releases of Debian, Ubuntu, RHEL-compatible systems (including AlmaLinux and Rocky Linux), SUSE (SLES and openSUSE Leap), Arch and Alpine for which fixed-version data exists. Releases without data (Debian 11 and older, Fedora, openSUSE Tumbleweed, derivatives other than those above) are shown as "unchecked", not as "no problem".
- **What it does not do**: it is not a complete vulnerability scan. Only entries in the KEV catalog that let a local user gain privileges are covered; vulnerabilities attacked remotely and application packages are not checked. It judges by version only, so it does not check whether the affected component is actually in use. For entries that need an extra condition (a SUID executable, or unprivileged user namespaces for regular users), a path appears only when the condition is met. For the kernel, only the running one is checked, not every installed kernel.
- **The data is bundled with the app.** Nothing is looked up over the network during an analysis. The result shows the date of the data, and warns when it is more than 45 days old. New data arrives with app updates.
- The screens and the command line were added in 10 languages (the translations are based on machine translation and have not been reviewed by native speakers).

The contents of these releases, grouped by theme. The full text of each entry is in this file's git history.

- **Added: PathScope can now be bundled in RoamSwitch's Linux packages (deb, rpm and tarball) on Linux.** It installs a launcher entry and icons so that it opens from the application menu, the `pathscope` command, the `pathscope-gui` window and the Apache-2.0 texts. An install made by a package does not use Settings > Update; it says that updates come with that package's updates. (0.1.4)
- **Fixed: on Linux (WebKitGTK) in dark mode, a closed drop-down kept a white background and its text was unreadable.** (0.1.4)
- **Fixed: depending on the startup order, the text in the update section could stay as a raw key name.** It could happen when the update information arrived before the text catalog was loaded (on both Mac and Linux). (0.1.4)
- **Changed: even with "unrestricted", private key files and API key or password values are not sent.** Until now, choosing "unrestricted" turned off both the replacement of values and the exclusion of files such as private keys. From now on, the following are kept whatever the setting. Private key files (a renamed key, or a key pasted into a config file, is found by its content, `-----BEGIN … PRIVATE KEY-----`). API keys in well-known formats (AWS, GitHub, Slack, OpenAI/Anthropic, Google, GitLab, npm, Stripe, JWT, `Bearer`), passwords inside URLs, and values whose name means a password or key (`name=value`, `name: value`, JSON, `--name value`) are replaced with `[REDACTED]`. What "unrestricted" still turns off is the exclusion of things like password hashes and `.env`, and the guess that treats long alphanumeric strings as secrets. Because this works by names and shapes, some secrets can still be missed. （0.1.2）
- **Fixed: the German label for "unrestricted" is now "Uneingeschränkt".** The earlier "Unbegrenzt" reads as "unlimited in quantity". The settings screen now matches the wording on the website and in the EULA. （0.1.3）
- **Fixed: even in standard mode, a renamed private key or a key pasted into a config file is no longer sent.** Files were excluded only by name and location, so a key with a name such as `backup.conf` could have part of its content sent (the replacement of long alphanumeric strings does not apply to lines that contain `/`). JSON `"password": "…"` and tokens that contain `.`, such as JWTs, are now replaced too. （0.1.2）
- **Fixed: with "unrestricted", the protection against instructions planted in config files (removing invisible characters, defusing the data fence marks) was also turned off.** It is now always applied, whatever the setting. （0.1.2）
- **Fixed: the result of "Fix everything?" is shown in plain sentences instead of internal wording.** （0.1.2）
- **Added: have an LLM read your config files to find easily missed risks (LLM assist).** Files such as sudoers, cron and scripts are written in many different styles, so a program alone cannot always read them. The LLM looks for entries that involve root privileges (for example, a sudo setting that needs no password, or a file that root runs periodically). Before anything it finds is used in the analysis, PathScope checks that the line really exists in the original file. Before sending, you confirm which LLM will receive how many files. Values that look like passwords or keys are replaced with `[REDACTED]`, and files such as private keys are not sent (for a local LLM whose content never leaves your machine, you can choose "unrestricted" in Settings). Files that could not be read or were not sent are listed as "unchecked" with the reason. The LLM integration is still off by default. (0.1.1)
- **Added: the app is available in 10 languages** (Japanese, English, Korean, Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese). It follows the OS language and can be changed in Settings → Language. The command line supports Japanese and English (`--lang=ja|en`). The translations started as machine translations and have not been reviewed by native speakers. The macOS screen names (System Settings, Privacy & Security, Full Disk Access) match the wording macOS itself uses in each language. (0.1.1)
- **Added: in-app updates on Linux.** The update is installed only after its signature is verified; updates with a wrong signature or a tampered file are refused. The Linux package (.deb) in this release is for delivering in-app updates; it is not yet offered as an installer. (0.1.1)
- **Fixed: the macOS privacy settings data (TCC.db) is now read correctly from real data**, including apps that are allowed by bundle ID. Commands in the suggested fixes are safely quoted even when the path contains spaces or symbols. (0.1.1)
- **Changed: the wording of the app and the command line is easier to understand** (the reason for each step is shown as a sentence instead of an internal name; "choke point" became "fix point", and so on). Settings is organized into tabs by function, overlapping text in the diagram is fixed, and a confirmation that explains the reason appears before opening the Full Disk Access settings. (0.1.1)
- **First release.** Bundled in the Mac 1.10.19 DMG. It analyzes, read-only, the paths by which privileges can be escalated on this machine (the routes to root or to sensitive data) and shows which places to fix first. (0.1.0)

## PersonalSOC

### 0.1.10

- **Added: consent to automatic log collection can now be given from the command line (`personalsoc consent`), for environments without the app window**. Until now consent could only be changed in the settings screen, so on Linux installed without a screen, and on the Server Edition, the "consent in Settings" that `personalsoc scan` asks for had nowhere to go. `personalsoc consent` lists what will be read (the sources enabled in this environment and the RoamSwitch MCP) and saves only when you answer `y` or pass `--yes`. Where it cannot ask and there is no `--yes`, it refuses and saves nothing. `--status` shows the current state and `--revoke` withdraws consent (scheduled runs stop too). Your other settings are kept as they are. Nothing is read until you consent, as before.
- **Changed: the consent error of `personalsoc scan` now points to `personalsoc consent`**.
- The new and changed strings are in 10 languages (other than Japanese and English they are machine translations and have not been checked by native speakers).

### 0.1.9

- **Added: known vulnerabilities in dependencies are imported through RoamSwitch.** PersonalSOC has RoamSwitch's `run_package_cve_scan_languages` match the project folders registered in RoamSwitch (its watched folders) and adds the known vulnerabilities of npm, PyPI, crates.io and other dependencies to the report. PersonalSOC passes no path and sends nothing. Because there can be many, only the 30 with the highest CVSS are listed and the rest is reported as a count. If no folder is registered, this is noted instead of being counted as a source failure.
- **Added: a ledger of confirmed facts in the LLM investigation.** Confirmed facts are built only from detections and tool output; nothing the LLM writes goes into them. The LLM's hypotheses are kept apart, shown under separate headings each round and in separate sections of the report. A new tool, `search_evidence`, searches only the collected log text with secrets masked, for a fixed string (not a regex; case-insensitive), and reads no files. Results are capped at 1 to 20 lines, 200 characters a line and about 4 KB in total. A search term appears in the facts only when it is a short plain token.
- **Added: the LLM can call `verify_security_findings`** (through RoamSwitch's MCP). It re-evaluates the diagnostic items right now and returns, per item, still present (stillPresent), resolved (resolved) or cannot be confirmed (inconclusive). An item that cannot be confirmed is counted as "unconfirmed", never as safe or resolved. Without RoamSwitch, or on an older version that lacks this tool, it returns an error and adds no fact. `run_active_vuln_scan` and `verify_fim` are still not allowed.
- **Changed: opencode is now treated as an LLM whose tools can be locked.** We confirmed with a live model that the shell, file creation, file read and edit, URL fetch and MCP tools all fail with a permission error, so the confirmation in Settings is no longer needed. Your own opencode settings can undo this lock, so before each run PersonalSOC checks the resolved permissions; if any allow rule remains, it does not start opencode and shows the rule that causes it.
- **Changed: claude is started without loading your settings** (`--setting-sources ""`), so that your hooks and CLAUDE.md files do not enter an investigation. A side effect is that a setup that signs in through `env` or `apiKeyHelper` in `~/.claude/settings.json` (a proxy, Bedrock, Vertex) fails with "Not logged in", and the error carries a hint. Adding the LLM's name to `llm_user_settings` in the settings file (hand-edited only) lets it read those settings; its tools and MCP stay switched off.
- **Changed: when an investigation reaches the round limit, the output is now the reason and the last hypothesis rather than raw JSON.**
- **Fixed: characters that act as line breaks, such as U+2028, are removed**, and search normalization now matches what is displayed.
- **Fixed: the third-party license list was rebuilt.** Dependencies such as the updater were missing from it. Only permissive licenses (MIT, Apache-2.0, ISC, BSD, Zlib) were added.
- The added texts are in 10 languages (8 of them machine-translated and not reviewed by native speakers).

### 0.1.0 - 0.1.6 (summarized)

- **0.1.0**: first release of "PersonalSOC", a personal security operations center that gathers this device's logs and defense status, read-only, into a report. Findings, sources and IOCs are shown with charts; the UI has 10 languages and follows the OS language. "Check status" has your chosen LLM CLI (opencode, claude, codex, agy) investigate autonomously with read-only tools and write an audit report. LLM integration is optional and off by default. PersonalSOC itself has no network permission.
- **0.1.1**: in-app updater (Mac), which fetches version information only when you press the button and verifies the signature before installing. Removed the fixed "Response" section from reports and fixed chart legends and axis labels overlapping in English and other languages.
- **0.1.2**: reads more from RoamSwitch's MCP (package CVE matches, active vulnerability check status, what Sensor found from outside, protections switched off, honeytoken, browser-credential and ransomware detections, recovery readiness, preserved evidence, Air-Gap). Gatekeeper denials on Mac became an optional source (off by default). ## RoamSwitch Sensor

Software you install on your own general-purpose hardware (PC, Raspberry
Pi, etc., x86_64/arm64) — a separate product: pairing-code-based mutual
trust (fixed IP + short-lived code) with RoamSwitch-equipped endpoints
(Mac / Linux Client / Server Edition) for active vulnerability audits,
plus detection of new devices and spoofing on the LAN. Still under
development — see
<https://lafine.net/roamswitch-sensor-manual.html> for current status and
setup instructions.

### 0.4.0

- **Added: detection of rogue DHCP servers (IPv4 and DHCPv6)**, on by default. Every hour (and on each `refresh`) the Sensor sends a single DHCP discover packet (a SOLICIT for DHCPv6) from a random MAC and counts every server that answers. It sends no request, so no address lease is taken. The first server it hears is learned as the legitimate one, and a server that appears later is reported. A server that hands out a gateway or DNS the legitimate one does not takes over where traffic goes, so it is critical. If several servers answer the first probe at once, none is learned and all are reported. `dhcp.allowed_servers` names the legitimate servers.
- **Added: IPv6 detection and support**, on by default. It reports router advertisements from anything other than the IPv4 gateway, an IPv6 address changing hands (NDP spoofing), and devices visible only over IPv6. The device inventory now holds IPv6 addresses (the `ipv6` CSV column, `ipv6_addrs` in JSON). The control API (50543) can also listen on IPv6 with `control.listen_ipv6` (off by default, applied on restart). Connection limits and lockouts count IPv6 per /64. A network whose IPv6 router is a different device from its IPv4 gateway is a normal case, so list that router in `ipv6.allowed_routers`.
- **Added: a light audit of devices without RoamSwitch**, opt-in and off by default. Only for devices inside the ranges the operator authorized, it makes TCP connections to a fixed set of 54 ports and reads what the services announce themselves. It does not attempt logins, exploit vulnerabilities, run nmap NSE or scan every port; deep diagnostics in the style of OpenVAS are done only to endpoints that agreed (paired). To enable it, first set `unmanaged_audit.authorization` (who approved it) and `unmanaged_audit.allowed_cidrs` (the ranges; IPv4 wider than /16 and IPv6 wider than /48 are refused). The device's MAC is checked before and after, and the run stops if the address changed hands. The authorization text is written to the operation log and the exports with every run. New: `audit-unmanaged <IP|MAC> --confirm` and `unmanaged-audits`, and in the TUI an "Unmanaged audits" tab and the `a` key.
- **Added: revocation and lockdown for a Sensor that is lost, stolen or retired.** After `revoke --reason` at the collector, that Sensor's uploads are refused with `403 sensor_revoked` (given only to requests with a valid signature), and the Sensor stops itself on its next upload. Locked down, it stops pairing, audits, uploads to the collector, notifications and probing of the network, and the control API answers only `sensor_locked_down`. The stop is stored in a file, so it survives a restart, and it stays in force if that file cannot be read. `security.on_revoked` chooses `lockdown` (default), `wipe` (overwrite with zeros and delete the keys, settings, paired endpoints, audit results and inventory, keeping only the operation log) or `none`. `security.lockdown_after_offline_days` stops a Sensor that has not reached the collector for that long (off by default). New: `lockdown on|off|status`, `decommission --confirm` and the `K` key in the TUI. `revoke` and `decommission` print the endpoints that were paired with the Sensor and how to unpair them.
- **Note: the endpoints that were paired with a revoked Sensor keep trusting it until each one removes the pairing.** A Sensor has no way to notify them (Linux: `roamswitch sensor unpair <this Sensor's public key>`; Mac: Settings > Sensor > Unpair). A Sensor carried off while powered down never receives the instruction to stop; disk encryption is the real protection.
- **Added: an `unmanaged` table in the evidence export** (CSV) and a light-audit section in the JSON and HTML reports. The inventory CSV has an IPv6 column. The collector dashboard lists revoked Sensors.
- **Verified with real packets.** On a throw-away Docker LAN, 35 checks cover DHCP and DHCPv6 servers, a rogue router advertisement, NDP spoofing, the light audit, the IPv6 listener, and revocation, lockdown, wiping and the offline stop (`scripts/lab/run.py`). It has not yet been checked under a real systemd (.deb) or on a physical switch. DHCP probing does not work on a Sensor connected over Wi-Fi; wired operation is assumed.
